⏺ This is a measurement problem before it's a methods problem, so let me be
  straight about the landscape: there is no ready-made public file that gives 
  you county-year diabetic retinopathy (DR) prevalence. CMS publishes
  county-level prevalence for diabetes, but not for DR as a separate condition.
  So you have two realistic paths, and which one you pick changes the whole
  project's cost and timeline.

  Path 1 (gold standard): build DR prevalence yourself from Medicare claims

  This is what a referee at a health-services or ophthalmology journal will
  expect for a causal design, and it's the only way to get DR specifically at
  county-year.

  Access: Medicare Research Identifiable Files (RIF) through ResDAC
  (resdac.org), under a Data Use Agreement. For a national county-year panel
  spanning the 1990s-2000s program openings, the CMS Virtual Research Data
  Center (VRDC) is usually better than physical extracts because the
  denominators (full beneficiary files) are enormous. Budget real money (often
  $15-40k+ depending on years/files) and several months for DUA approval.

  Files you'd pull:
  - Carrier (Part B) and Outpatient files for the diagnoses (most DR is
  diagnosed/treated in office settings, so carrier is essential, not just
  inpatient).
  - Master Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF) for the denominator and the residence
  county (it carries beneficiary ZIP/county and FFS-vs-MA enrollment).

  Defining a DR case (ICD diagnosis on a claim):
  - ICD-9 era (your 1995-2000 openings live here): 362.01-362.07 (background,
  proliferative, NPDR severities, diabetic macular edema) and 250.5x (diabetes
  with ophthalmic manifestations).
  - ICD-10 era (post Oct 2015): E08.3-, E09.3-, E10.3-, E11.3-, E13.3- (diabetes
  with retinopathy/ophthalmic complications, including DME).

  Denominator choice (this is the modeling decision that matters most):
  - DR prevalence among diabetics = DR cases / beneficiaries with a diabetes
  diagnosis. This isolates "given diabetes, is retinopathy being
  caught/managed," which is closer to what an ophthalmology program would
  plausibly move.
  - DR prevalence in the population = DR cases / all FFS beneficiaries. Cleaner
  denominator but confounded by underlying diabetes prevalence.

  I'd build both.

  County assignment: use beneficiary residence county from the MBSF (mapped from
  ZIP via the HUD-USPS crosswalk for the year), not the provider's county. You
  care about where patients live, not where the program sits.

  One thing to decide up front: restrict to FFS only and exclude Medicare
  Advantage beneficiary-years, because MA encounter data is incomplete for
  claims-based case-finding. This biases your sample toward FFS counties/years,
  worth a robustness check given MA penetration rose sharply over your window.

  Path 2 (fast and free, but weaker): public aggregates

  If you want to scope the project or run a first-pass before committing to a
  DUA:

  - CDC VEHSS (Vision and Eye Health Surveillance System) actually has diabetic 
  retinopathy prevalence estimates built from Medicare, Medicaid, MarketScan,
  and the IRIS registry. The catch: it's mostly national and state level, not a
  clean county-year panel. Worth checking their county releases for your outcome
  years.
  - CMS Mapping Medicare Disparities tool / Chronic Conditions PUF: county-year
  prevalence of diabetes (not DR) among FFS beneficiaries. Usable as a
  covariate/denominator proxy, or as a placebo/upstream outcome, but not your DR
  measure.
  - CDC PLACES / US Diabetes Surveillance: county diabetes and "vision
  disability," again not DR specifically.

  Realistically Path 2 cannot deliver your dependent variable. It's good for
  denominators and validation.

  Two design caveats specific to your setup

  1. Medicare is 65+. A residency program affects screening and treatment
  capacity across all ages, but your claims-based outcome will only capture the
  65+ FFS population unless you add Medicaid (T-MSIS) or commercial
  (MarketScan/Optum) data. DR is heavily an under-65 working-age disease too.
  State this scope limit explicitly; it's defensible but it narrows the claim.
  2. Treatment intensity may be a better-powered outcome than raw prevalence. A
  new program plausibly raises detection and treatment before it lowers
  prevalence. Consider claims-based treatment outcomes alongside prevalence:
  anti-VEGF intravitreal injections (CPT 67028 + drug J-codes for
  aflibercept/ranibizumab/bevacizumab), panretinal photocoagulation (67228),
  focal laser (67210), vitrectomy (67036-67043). "More treated DR after a
  program opens" and "lower late-stage/proliferative share over time" are
  cleaner, faster-moving signals than prevalence and tell a more credible
  mechanism story.

  One more thing worth flagging now: most of your accreditation dates cluster in
  1995-2000, so a lot of "treated" counties adopt nearly simultaneously. That
  limits clean never-treated comparisons and pushes you toward later-opening
  programs (Dell 2021, UNM 2023, Phoenix 2024, etc.) as the identifying
  variation. Given your Chapter 3 work, you're already in Callaway/Sant'Anna
  territory, and that staggered-with-clustering structure is exactly where
  group-time ATT estimators earn their keep.

  sketch the ResDAC data request first?

⏺ Good instinct to name NHANES, but let me kill that one first so you don't sink
  time into it, then build out what free data can actually do.

  Why NHANES can't carry this design

  NHANES did do retinal photography with graded DR (notably the 2005-2008
  cycles), but two things make it unusable here: (1) the public files have no 
  geographic identifier below census region, so there is no county, and getting
  sub-state geography means the NCHS Research Data Center, which is paid and
  restricted, the exact wall you're trying to avoid; and (2) even with
  geography, the per-county sample size is effectively zero. NHANES answers
  "national DR prevalence," not "county-year panel." Same fate for any survey
  with a clinical DR exam.

  The reframe that makes a zero-budget version possible

  Free data will not hand you DR prevalence at county-year. What it can give
  you, all free, is the causal chain around DR:

  program opens → local ophthalmologist supply rises → diabetic eye-exam 
  (screening) rises → progression to vision-threatening/surgical DR falls.

  You can measure the first link, the screening link, and the severe-outcome
  link for free. That's arguably a stronger paper than a single noisy prevalence
  regression, because you show the mechanism, not just a reduced-form blob.

  The free menu, by link in the chain

  1. Treatment / first stage (does a program actually add local eye-care 
  capacity?) — AHRF
  The Area Health Resources File (HRSA) is free, county-year, back to the early
  1990s, and carries ophthalmologist counts (from the AMA Masterfile). Use it to
  validate your treatment: did counties that got a program actually gain
  ophthalmologists, and when? This is your first stage and a falsification check
  in one. NPPES (NPI registry, free, 2007+) corroborates for the recent
  openings.

  2. Screening mechanism — Dartmouth Atlas
  Free. Reports the rate of diabetic Medicare enrollees (65-75) receiving an
  annual eye exam, by HRR/HSA, in a time series. This is the cleanest free "did
  detection improve" measure. Geography is HRR/HSA, not county, but those
  crosswalk to counties.

  3. Severe / surgical DR outcome — free state discharge & ambulatory-surgery 
  databases
  This is the creative workhorse. Several states publish all-payer, 
  patient-county-coded, ICD/CPT-coded discharge and outpatient-surgery data for
  free:
  - Texas (DSHS/THCIC PUDF, inpatient from ~1999) — and you're at UT Austin, so
  the Dell Medical 2021 Austin opening is right there.
  - California (HCAI, formerly OSHPD, back to the 1990s), New York (SPARCS, on
  health.data.ny.gov), Florida (AHCA), Washington (CHARS), among others.

  From these you build, by patient county-year: rate of DR-related surgery
  (vitrectomy 67036-67043, panretinal photocoagulation 67228) and inpatient
  diabetes-with-ophthalmic-complications. Facility data under-captures
  office-based diagnosis and anti-VEGF, but it cleanly captures severe, 
  progression-to-surgery DR, which is exactly the outcome a screening program
  should reduce. "Fewer vitrectomies per diabetic after a local program opens"
  is a publishable, mechanism-consistent result.

  4. Free CMS county aggregates (covariates + a partial outcome) — Chronic 
  Conditions PUF / Provider Utilization PUF
  Free, county-year, ~2007+. No DR line item, but you get county diabetes
  prevalence (denominator), glaucoma/cataract as eye-care-utilization proxies,
  and from the Provider Utilization & Payment PUF (2012+) provider-side counts
  of 67028 anti-VEGF injections and 67228 laser by provider county.

  5. BRFSS diabetes module — a free self-reported DR outcome
  BRFSS has, as an optional module, "has a doctor ever told you diabetes
  affected your eyes / you had retinopathy?" Free, multiple state-years, with
  county FIPS for larger counties (small counties suppressed). Self-reported and
  patchy, but it's a genuine county-ish DR outcome at zero cost. Good as a
  triangulation/robustness outcome.

  6. CDC VEHSS / PLACES — free; VEHSS has modeled DR prevalence but mostly
  state-level, PLACES has county diabetes. Use for validation and denominators,
  not as your main DV.

  The constraint that should actually drive your strategy

  Here's the binding issue, and it's about time coverage, not DR specifically:
  most of your accreditation dates cluster in 1995-2000, but the free 
  CMS/Dartmouth panels don't start until ~2007. For the 1990s cohort, those
  sources give you no pre-period, which is fatal for DiD. That splits the free
  project cleanly into two coherent designs:

  - Strategy A (recent cohort, free CMS/PLACES/Dartmouth): restrict to post-2007
  openings (Stony Brook 2007, the 2016-2022 community programs, Dell 2021, UNM
  2023, Phoenix 2024, ECU 2025, UMass/Dartmouth 2019). These have clean free
  pre-periods. Fewer treated units, but the data is free and the parallel-trends
  story is checkable.
  - Strategy B (older cohort, free state discharge data): the state databases
  reach back into the 1990s, so they're the only free source with a real
  pre-period for the 1995-2000 openings. You give up national coverage (you get
  the states that publish), but you get the surgical-DR outcome with a true
  before/after.

  Given your Chapter 3 Callaway/Sant'Anna setup, both strategies are
  staggered-adoption-friendly, and Strategy A with the recent never-treated-yet
  counties is the most defensible group-time ATT version.

  If I had to pick a free stack: AHRF (treatment validation) + Dartmouth 
  eye-exam (mechanism) + one or two free state ASC databases including Texas 
  (surgical DR outcome) + BRFSS (triangulation), run on the post-2007 cohort
  first.

❯ is there a best unified way instead of two piecemeal? please let me know the  
  best methods to try to get at this conceptual problem                         

⏺ Short version: there is no single free source that is simultaneously national,
  DR-specific, county-resolved, and long enough to cover both your 1990s and
  your 2020s openings. So "unified" can't come from finding a magic file. It has
  to come from committing to one data spine and one geographic frame and
  accepting the scope that implies. The good news is that one combination
  dominates the others, and the thing that makes it unified is a conceptual
  choice, not a data choice.

  The unifying move: change the unit from county to health-care market

  The deepest problem in your question is hiding in the word "county." A
  residency program does not serve its home county. It serves a referral region,
  and patients (especially for eye surgery) cross county lines constantly. If
  you code treatment at the county and outcomes at the county, you get two
  failures at once: you understate exposure (treated patients live in
  neighboring counties), and you violate SUTVA (your "control" counties are
  absorbing spillover care from the program). Both bias you toward zero.

  Fix both at the root by making the unit the Hospital Referral Region (HRR) (or
  a commuting zone). One geographic frame then does four things simultaneously:
  - absorbs cross-border patient flow, so SUTVA holds at the market level,
  - matches how a training program actually distributes capacity (graduates and
  faculty practice across the region),
  - lets you aggregate any free source (state discharge data, AHRF, Dartmouth,
  BRFSS) up to the same geography, which is what kills the piecemeal feel, and
  - aligns with the Dartmouth screening measure for free mechanism evidence.

  That single decision is what turns four scattered datasets into one coherent
  panel: everything rolls up to HRR-year.

  The recommended unified design

  Spine (the outcome): free state all-payer discharge + ambulatory-surgery 
  databases. This is the only free source that is DR-specific, patient-residence
  coded, and reaches back into the 1990s, so it covers both your old and recent
  cohorts in one data model. Build a single outcome: the rate of
  vision-threatening / surgical DR (vitrectomy 67036-67043, panretinal
  photocoagulation 67228, plus inpatient diabetes-with-ophthalmic-complications)
  per diabetic (or per capita), aggregated to HRR-year. Commit to the states
  that publish these for free (Texas, California, New York, Florida, Washington,
  and a few others) and define those states as your study universe. That is not
  a compromise to apologize for, it is your population, and it still contains a
  large share of your treated programs across both cohorts (all the Texas
  programs including Dell, the California cluster, the many New York programs,
  Florida, UW).

  Treatment + first stage: AHRF. Map each accreditation to its HRR, and use
  AHRF's county ophthalmologist counts (aggregated to HRR) to prove the first 
  stage: programs that opened actually raised local ophthalmologist supply, and
  when. This is one extra free file used only to validate the treatment, so it
  does not reintroduce the piecemeal problem.

  Estimator: Callaway–Sant'Anna event study at HRR-year, your Chapter 3 toolkit,
  with not-yet-treated HRRs as controls.

  One clean outcome, one geography, one estimator, one study population. That is
  the unified version.

  Why this beats the two single-source alternatives

  - National Medicare Provider Utilization PUF (2012+): tempting because it's
  free, national, and has anti-VEGF (67028) and laser counts. But it's
  provider-county not patient-county, and starts in 2012, so it can only see
  your handful of most-recent openings and conflates where care is delivered
  with where patients live. Breadth, but no history and the wrong geography.
  - Dartmouth Atlas: free and already HRR-level, but it measures screening, not
  DR, and the series is short. Perfect as your mechanism layer inside the design
  above, not as the spine.

  The conceptual issues you must nail (these matter more than the data plumbing)

  1. Catchment definition of treatment. HRR is the default; as a robustness
  check, define treatment by a distance/donut (program within X miles), and
  exclude immediately-adjacent untreated markets from the control pool to avoid
  spillover contamination.
  2. Ramp-up timing. A residency's effect is not a step function. Residents take
  ~4 years to graduate and supply builds gradually, so specify the event study
  to allow a lagged, growing effect and inspect the dynamic path rather than a
  single post coefficient.
  3. Selection on trends. Programs open at existing academic medical centers in
  places with more disease and more care, so levels will never be comparable.
  Lean entirely on pre-trends and not-yet-treated controls, and show the
  screening (Dartmouth) and supply (AHRF) first stages move before the surgical
  outcome does.
  4. Denominator. Prefer surgical DR per diabetic (use PLACES/CMS county
  diabetes prevalence, rolled to HRR) over per capita, so you're measuring care
  quality given disease, not just disease burden.

  If you want to start, the cleanest first build is the AHRF treatment file: map
  all 100+ accreditations to HRRs and verify the supply first stage, because if
  programs don't measurably raise local ophthalmologist counts, the whole
  causal chain is dead before you touch outcomes. Want me to build that
  program-to-HRR treatment-and-first-stage file from your accreditation list?

  The collision: the big cohort is the unusable one

  Callaway–Sant'Anna needs, for every treated group, at least one pre-treatment 
  period and a clean comparison pool (never-treated or not-yet-treated). The
  1994–2000 cluster fails both, for reasons that compound:

  1. No pre-period in free data. National free outcomes start ~2007. Even in the
  long-history states, most of these programs predate or sit at the data floor,
  so there's little or no "before."
  2. No clean controls during the window. Roughly 70% of your programs treat
  inside a 7-year span. By 2000 essentially every academic/metro market is
  treated, so your only never-treated units are rural markets with no AMC, which
  are not credible counterfactuals for places that host medical schools.
  3. CS literally drops always-treated units, so a 1996 program with data
  starting 2007 contributes nothing as a treated group.

  So the 89-program cohort is dead weight for a credible CS design. That is the
  hard truth, and it's a coverage problem, not something a fancier estimator
  fixes.

  Does opening an ophthalmology residency reduce diabetic retinopathy? Project 
  summary

  The question

  When a county (or region) gets a new academic ophthalmology training program,
  does diabetic retinopathy (DR) burden go down? The idea: a new program adds
  eye-care capacity, which should mean more diabetic eye screening, earlier
  detection, and fewer people progressing to vision-threatening disease.

  The core problem

  There is no ready-made, free dataset of county-level DR prevalence over time.
  CMS publishes county diabetes rates, but not DR specifically. The
  gold-standard option (raw Medicare claims) costs real money and needs a
  data-use agreement, which we don't have budget for. NHANES won't work either:
  it has no county identifier and far too few people per county.

  So we cannot simply download "DR rate by county by year." We have to build the
  outcome from free sources, and accept some trade-offs.

  What we can measure for free

  We can't measure DR prevalence directly, but we can measure the chain of 
  events around it, which actually tells a stronger clinical story:

  1. Did the program add eye-care capacity? (ophthalmologist counts by area,
  free, back to ~1990)
  2. Did diabetic eye screening go up? (Medicare diabetic eye-exam rates, free)
  3. Did severe/surgical DR go down? (rates of vitrectomy, laser, and
  diabetes-eye-complication hospitalizations, built from free state hospital and
  surgery databases)

  Showing all three move in sequence (capacity up, screening up, severe disease
  down) is more convincing than a single prevalence number.

  The recommended approach, in plain terms

  - Compare regions that got a new program to similar regions that didn't,
  before vs after the program opened. This is a difference-in-differences design
  (the same logic as a before/after study with a control group).
  - Use the region (referral area), not the county, as the unit. A training
  program serves a whole catchment, and patients cross county lines for eye
  surgery. Using the broader region avoids miscounting who was actually
  affected.
  - The specific tool is a modern difference-in-differences estimator
  (Callaway–Sant'Anna) built for situations where different places get "treated"
  in different years. It's the same family of methods we're using in the other
  dissertation chapter.

  A key requirement of this method: for every program we study, we need data 
  from before it opened (a baseline), and we need comparison regions that are
  genuinely similar.

  The catch (this drives everything)

  Here is the central tension between our program list and the available data:

  - Most programs (~89 of ~127) were accredited in 1994–2000. But free
  DR-relevant data mostly starts around 2007. So for these older programs we
  have little or no "before" period, and by 2000 almost every academic/metro
  area already had a program, leaving no good comparison regions. This cohort is
  essentially unusable for a credible causal analysis.
  - About 18 programs opened in 2005–2025. These have a clean baseline in the
  free data and open in different years, which is exactly what the method needs.

  How many programs we can actually use

  After requiring both a baseline and enough follow-up time (a residency's
  effect takes ~4 years to build, since that's when the first residents finish),
  the usable set is roughly:

  - ~10 to 13 distinct regions with new programs in 2005–2022 (e.g., Stony Brook
  '07, the Detroit-area and Philadelphia community programs '16–'21, several
  Florida programs '18–'19, UMass and Dartmouth '19, Dell/Austin '21).
  - The very recent ones (2023–2025) have no meaningful "after" period yet, so
  they serve as additional comparison regions for now.

  Is 10–13 enough? Yes for estimating an overall average effect, comparable to
  many published studies using this method. It is not enough to reliably break
  the effect down program-by-program, and we'll need a specific statistical
  correction for having a small number of "treated" regions (otherwise the
  results look more certain than they are).

  Bottom line

  - We can do this credibly and for free, but the study is realistically a
  ~10–13 region analysis of programs opened 2005–2022, not a 127-program study.
  - The most defensible version measures the mechanism (more eye doctors, more
  screening, less severe DR), not raw prevalence.
  - To get more statistical power, our best levers are: (a) count every new
  program in the window, not just the flagship academic ones, and (b) consider a
  faster-moving outcome like screening/treatment rates rather than waiting for
  prevalence to shift.

  Decisions we need to make together

  1. Outcome: severe/surgical DR (slower to move, more clinically meaningful) vs
  screening/treatment rates (faster, better powered)? We can do both, but which
  is primary?
  2. Scope: national (free CMS data, 2007+) vs one or two data-rich states with
  longer history (e.g., California)?
  3. What counts as "treated": flagship academic programs only, or every new
  ophthalmology program (recommended, for power)?
